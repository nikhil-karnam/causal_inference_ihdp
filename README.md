  an implementation and evaluation of a T-learner (a CATE estimator) for causal inference, benchmarked on the semi-synthetic IHDP dataset. heterogeneity in treatment effect (HTE) was analyzed using the BLP / GATES / CLAN framework from Chernozhukov et al., pooled across 100 Monte Carlo replications. histograms demonstrate skewed distributions across reps, justifying reporting the median. qini curves visualize cumulative gain in outcome as the participants with the predicted highest response are treated first.


background:
in causal inference, treatment effect is defined as an individual's potential outcome when treated ($Y_1$) minus the potential outcome when untreated ($Y_0$). ATE (average treatment effect) is $Y_1 - Y_0$ averaged across all individuals. and CATE (conditional average treatment effect) is averaged across those who non-negotiably share a certain trait value or exact set of trait values.

the ihdp dataset comes from an randomized controlled experiment in the 1980s. jennifer hill later turned this dataset into synthetic observational data to help study causal machine learning. she did this by artificially introducing a confounder: removing non-white mothers in the treated group. a traditional confounder is unevenly distributed across groups because it affects the treatment assignment, and the presence of the confounder also affects the outcome. here, the treated group being white is associated with socioeconomic advantages that affect the outcome variable in the study. thus, the outcome variable is artificially inflated, and a naive difference in mean outcomes results in a biased treatment effect estimate. the goal of CATE estimators in causal machine learning is to say "instead of a biased  difference in means, let's compare the outcome between individuals that share the same background but differ purely in treatment status, in an attempt to eliminate the effect of confounding." controlling for confounders is what allows inferring causality to be more legal. hence, the name.
